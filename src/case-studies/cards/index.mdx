## Project landscape

<Prose>

Neuflize OBC is ABN AMRO's private bank in France. Each client has a dedicated relationship manager — a personal banker who advises on investments, estate planning, and financial strategy.

Card management — adjusting limits, blocking lost cards, toggling payments abroad — had no self-service path. Every change went through the RM or the operations team.

</Prose>

## The business goal

<Prose>

Relationship managers are the bank's highest-value resource — financial advisors whose time generates revenue. But a significant share of their client interactions were routine card requests: raise a limit, block a card, enable payments abroad.

These requests got redirected to the operations team, who processed each change manually. The bank was spending advisory-level cost on clerical work, clients waited hours for changes that took minutes to execute, and the ops team carried the volume with no system support.

The goal: **make routine card operations self-service** — reduce operational cost, free RM capacity for the advisory work that justifies a private bank, and give clients direct control over tasks that never needed a human in the loop.

</Prose>

## The problem

<Prose>

Clients had no way to manage their own debit cards. Every change — a limit bump, a block, payments abroad — meant contacting the RM and waiting.

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="user">Clients weren't asking for anything clever. They wanted to change a limit or block a card — and felt powerless that they couldn't.</PullQuote>

Every step was a handoff, every handoff a place it could stall. The fix was not to speed the chain — it was to remove it.

</Prose>

## How I approached it

<Prose>

I interviewed clients, relationship managers, operations, and digital support to find where friction actually lived. Both sides agreed: clients felt powerless over trivial tasks, and internal teams knew the requests were routine but still had to process every one manually.

Before drawing anything, I ran a **workshop with stakeholders** — mapping tasks clients actually did against problems they actually hit.

</Prose>

<ImageFigure
  src={`${IMAGES}/04-workshop.png`}
  alt="A whiteboard from the discovery workshop: clustered sticky notes under Usage and Problem columns, with How might we questions written alongside."
  caption="Mapping usage against real problems — the input that decided what to build first, and what to leave for later."
/>

<Prose>

Four problems rose to the top — by frequency and how cleanly they could go self-service:

- **Limit change** — the single most common request
- **Block / unblock** — urgent and time-sensitive
- **Card removal** — rare, but entirely manual
- **Payments abroad** — recurring friction for travelling clients

Everything else stayed out of the first release on purpose. Choosing four and defending the boundary mattered as much as the design itself.

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="principle">
  <strong>Self-service doesn't mean removing friction everywhere.</strong> It means putting friction exactly where the <em>risk</em> is — and nowhere else.
</PullQuote>

<Prose>

Two surfaces carried the most design weight: the **card landing page** (what can I do here, is my card okay?) and the **limit-change flow** (how much, for how long, prove it is you). I explored both widely before committing.

</Prose>

<DecisionBlock

  title="Authenticate at the point of commitment — not on the way in"

    why: (

      </>
    ),
  }}
>

Viewing your card and flipping low-risk switches — remote payment, cash withdrawal, payments abroad — happen instantly. The identity check appears only when the action would actually cost the client if it were the wrong person.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock
  index="04"
  tag="Compliance"
  title="Default the limit change to temporary, not permanent"
  tradeoff={{
    considered: 'A single permanent "new limit" field.',
    why: (
      <>
        Most limit bumps are for one reason — a large purchase, a trip — not a permanent change. A permanent-only field
        either over-exposes the client or forces a second call to revert. Temporary with an auto-revert date fits what
        clients actually need.
      </>
    ),
  }}
>

Duration is part of the request: raise the limit for seven days or thirty, up to a shown ceiling, and it reverts automatically. The card shows a "temporarily valid until" note — no surprise state. Identity verified with a PIN step folded into the flow.

</DecisionBlock>

A **Call customer service** shortcut sits at the bottom of every screen — the client is always one tap from a person, even mid-flow. Flows that need verified contact details check upfront; if details are missing, the app routes to the bank first instead of dead-ending at the last step.

</DecisionBlock>

## Built on Emerald

<Prose>

Every screen is built from **ABN AMRO's Emerald design system** — the bank's shared component library. I owned the flows and decisions; the system carried consistency and cut build cost.

The digital cards deliberately **mirror the physical ones** — each product's real colour and pattern (Visa Infinite, Premier, Business). A client finds their card by its look, not by reading the number. Recognition, not recall.

</Prose>

## What we kept with a human, on purpose

<Prose>

Two actions deliberately stayed manual: **permanently cancelling a card** and **editing contact details** on file. Cancellation cannot be undone; letting a client edit the details used to verify their identity would defeat the check.

In a private bank, the right first release is not the one that automates the most — it is the one that automates every action where self-service is clearly safer and faster, and knows exactly where to stop.

</Prose>

## The takeaway

<Prose>

This was not a task made prettier. It was a **handoff chain collapsed into a flow the client runs alone** — and the discipline that made it safe was refusing to spread friction evenly.

On a regulated product, self-service is a question of placement, not addition. Put the guardrail exactly where the risk is, make everything else instant, and know precisely which actions still belong with a human.

</Prose>
