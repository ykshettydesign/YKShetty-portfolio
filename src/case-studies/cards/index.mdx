## The outcome

<Prose>

At Neuflize OBC — ABN AMRO's private bank in France — clients had **no way to manage their own debit cards**. Adjusting a limit, blocking a lost card, turning on payments abroad: each one meant contacting a relationship manager and waiting.

The feature I designed is **live in production** on native iOS and Android. It takes a change that used to run through two people and up to two hours, and resolves it in a few taps — while keeping every check a private bank needs.

</Prose>

<Prose>

*One principle held the whole thing together:*

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="principle">
  <strong>Self-service doesn't mean removing friction everywhere.</strong> It means putting friction exactly where the <em>risk</em> is — and nowhere else.
</PullQuote>

## The problem

<Prose>

Picture being a private-banking client who wants to raise a card's spending limit before a big purchase. You can't do it yourself. You call — or email — your relationship manager, the personal banker every client is assigned. Eventually they pick up. You explain. They pass it to the operations team, who open the bank's internal system and make the change by hand, then report back up the chain to you.

Nothing about that request was complex. The wait was **entirely structural** — the client simply had no door into the system.

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="user">Clients weren't asking for anything clever. They wanted to change a limit or block a card — and felt powerless that they couldn't.</PullQuote>

<Prose>

What that cost, measured across the operation:

- A client could wait **up to two hours** for a change as small as a limit bump.
- RMs fielded **2,000+ card requests a year** — routine work pulling them off the advisory conversations they're actually there for.
- Operations spent roughly **80 hours a week** processing those requests by hand, acting as a manual layer between the client and a system that could have taken the instruction directly.

The core question was blunt: *could card management become fully self-service — without a private bank giving up any of its control?*

</Prose>

<Prose>

The pattern was the whole problem: **every step was a handoff, and every handoff was a place it could stall.** The fix wasn't to speed the chain up — it was to remove it, and let the client do the whole thing themselves.

</Prose>

## How I approached it

<Prose>

I started with interviews on both sides of the counter — clients, bankers, operations, digital support — to find where the friction actually lived, not where the business assumed it did. Both sides agreed: clients felt powerless over trivial tasks, and the internal teams knew these requests were routine but still had to touch every one.

Then, before drawing anything, I ran a **workshop with stakeholders** — mapping the tasks clients actually did against the problems they actually hit, so we built for real behaviour, not an assumed feature list.

</Prose>

<ImageFigure
  src={`${IMAGES}/04-workshop.png`}
  alt="A whiteboard from the discovery workshop: clustered sticky notes under Usage and Problem columns, with ‘How might we…’ questions written alongside for lost cards, managing multiple cards, changing limits, forgotten PINs and payment errors."
  caption="Mapping usage against real problems — the input that decided what to build first, and what to leave for later."
/>

<Prose>

That mapping is where the **scope decision** got made. Four problems rose to the top — on how often they happened, and how cleanly they could go self-service:

- **Limit change** — the single most common request
- **Block / unblock** — urgent and time-sensitive by nature
- **Card removal** — rare, but entirely manual today
- **Payments abroad** — recurring friction for travelling clients

Everything else stayed out of the first release on purpose. Choosing four and defending the boundary mattered as much as the design itself.

**One boundary worth naming:** I owned the client-facing design — the flows and the screens. The bank's back-end systems — the ones that set the limits, hold the card data, and check a client's identity — belonged to engineering, and the whole idea depended on the app being able to reach them. Knowing where my slice started and ended shaped every trade-off below.

</Prose>

## The hard part was translation, not requirements

<Prose>

I'll be honest about where the difficulty was. The *ask* was never ambiguous: make card management self-service, keep it bank-grade secure. The hard part was the **design** — turning a task that had always been handled by a person, with all the checking and reassurance that person quietly provided, into a screen a client trusts enough to act on alone.

Two surfaces carried most of that weight, and I explored both widely before committing. **The card landing page** had to answer a client's first two questions at a glance — *what can I do here, and is my card okay?* **The limit-change flow** had to hold a lot at once — how much, for how long, and proof it's really you — without feeling like the phone call it replaced.

</Prose>

Before the client types anything, the limit screen shows what they've already used and the ceiling they're allowed to reach. It's the same "state first" instinct as the landing page, applied to the riskiest input in the feature. This was the fix for the most common error we saw — clients setting a limit without understanding its bounds, then calling to sort it out afterwards.

<DecisionBlock
  index="03"
  tag="Security"
  title="Authenticate at the point of commitment — not on the way in"
  media={{

  tradeoff={{
    considered: 'Gating the whole feature behind a verification wall — the compliance-safe instinct to lock every action.',
    why: (
      <>
        Uniform friction would have rebuilt the exact wait we were removing. Most card actions are low-risk and
        reversible; a few aren't. So I put the identity step where the <strong>commitment</strong> is — raising a
        limit, deleting a card — and left browsing, viewing and toggling free.
      </>
    ),
  }}
>

Viewing your card and flipping low-risk switches — remote payment, cash withdrawal, payments abroad — happen instantly. The identity check appears only when a client is about to do something that would actually cost them if it were the wrong person. Same feature, friction matched to the stakes.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock
  index="04"
  tag="Compliance"
  title="Default the limit change to temporary, not permanent"
  tradeoff={{
    considered: 'A single permanent “new limit” field, the simplest possible control.',
    why: (
      <>
        Watching the real requests, most limit bumps are for one reason — a large purchase, a trip — not a permanent
        change. A permanent-only field either over-exposes the client indefinitely or forces a <em>second</em> call
        to put it back. Foregrounding a temporary increase with an automatic end date fit what clients actually needed.
      </>
    ),
  }}
>

The flow makes duration part of the request: a client raises the limit *for seven days*, or *for thirty*, up to a clearly shown ceiling, and it reverts on its own. The card screen then shows a quiet “temporarily valid until…” note, so the temporary state is never a surprise. Identity is proven with a PIN step folded into the flow — not a separate hoop.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock
  index="06"
  tag="Trust"
  title="Keep the human reachable — and hand off gracefully"
>

Self-service should never feel like abandonment — least of all in private banking, where the relationship-manager bond carries real value. So a **Call customer service shortcut sits at the bottom of every screen**: the client is always one tap from a person, even mid-flow.

The same instinct shows up in how the flows fail safely. A sensitive change needs verified contact details — so the app checks up front, and if they're missing, it sends the client to the bank to fix that first, instead of dead-ending them at the last step.

The vast majority of cases now resolve in the app. This decision is for the ones that don't: give them a clean exit, not a dead end.

</DecisionBlock>

## Built on Emerald

<Prose>

None of this shipped as custom, one-off design. Every screen is built from **ABN AMRO's Emerald design system** — the bank's shared library of ready-made, approved components — so the feature feels part of the wider app, and it reached development faster because engineers were assembling known pieces rather than interpreting new ones.

That's the quiet leverage of working inside a system: I owned the *flows and the decisions*; the system carried the consistency and cut the build cost. On a regulated banking app, that's not a constraint to work around — it's what makes the work shippable.

One piece of craft worth calling out: the digital cards deliberately **mirror the physical ones** — each product's real colour and pattern (Visa Infinite, Premier, Business…). A client finds *their* card in the list by its look, not by reading the number. Recognition, not recall — a small thing that removes a real moment of friction every time they open the app.

</Prose>

## What we kept with a human, on purpose

<Prose>

The honest boundary of this release: **not every card action became self-service, and that was a design choice, not a gap I missed.**

Two things still route to a person — **permanently cancelling a card**, and **fixing a wrong email or phone number** on file. Both are irreversible or identity-sensitive: cancellation can't be undone, and letting a client edit the very details used to verify them would defeat the check. The seconds saved by automating either aren't worth the risk.

Both are deliberate. In a private bank, the right first release isn't the one that automates the most actions — it's the one that automates every action where doing it yourself is clearly *safer and faster*, and knows exactly where to stop. Naming that line is the point, not apologising for it.

</Prose>

## The impact, in the operation

<Prose>

The numbers up top are the operation's own — measured internally, not estimated by me, and the reason the project got prioritised. But the bigger change was in *who does the work*: bankers got hours back for the advisory conversations that justify a private bank, operations stopped being a manual relay, and clients got something a phone call never gave them — the ability to act the moment they needed to.

It also set a pattern — proving a genuinely sensitive banking task could be made self-service, safely, cleared the way for the next ones.

</Prose>

## The takeaway

<Prose>

This wasn't a task made prettier. It was a **handoff chain collapsed into a flow the client runs alone** — and the discipline that made it safe to do was refusing to spread friction evenly.

The clearest lesson: on a regulated product, self-service is a question of *placement*, not addition. Put the guardrail exactly where the risk is, make everything else instant, and know — precisely — which few actions still belong with a human. That's what turns a two-hour call into a few taps without a bank giving up an ounce of control.

</Prose>
