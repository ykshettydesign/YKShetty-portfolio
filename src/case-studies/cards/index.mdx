## The outcome

<Prose>

At Neuflize OBC — ABN AMRO's private bank in France — clients had **no way to manage their own debit cards**. Adjusting a limit, blocking a lost card, turning on payments abroad: each one meant contacting a relationship manager and waiting.

The feature I designed is **live in production** on native iOS and Android. It takes a change that used to run through two people and up to two hours, and resolves it in a few taps — while keeping every check a private bank needs.

</Prose>

*One principle held the whole thing together:*

</Prose>

## The problem

<Prose>

Picture being a private-banking client who wants to raise a card limit before a large purchase. You can't just do it. You call — or email — your relationship manager (RM). They pick up, eventually. You explain. They pass it to the operations team, who log into the card-management back office and make the change by hand, then tell the RM, who tells you it's done.

Nothing about that request was complex. The wait was **entirely structural** — the client simply had no door into the system.

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="user">Clients weren't asking for anything clever. They wanted to change a limit or block a card — and felt powerless that they couldn't.</PullQuote>

<Prose>

What that cost, measured across the operation:

- A client could wait **up to two hours** for a change as small as a limit bump.
- RMs fielded **2,000+ card requests a year** — routine work pulling them off the advisory conversations they're actually there for.
- Operations spent roughly **80 hours a week** processing those requests by hand, acting as a manual layer between the client and a system that could have taken the instruction directly.

The core question was blunt: *could card management become fully self-service — without a private bank giving up any of its control?*

</Prose>

  wide

<Prose>

Laid out end to end, the diagnosis was obvious: **every step was a handoff, and every handoff was a failure point** — a person to reach, a queue to wait in, a verbal instruction that could be misheard, and no status the client could see. The job wasn't to speed the chain up. It was to collapse it into a single self-contained flow the client runs alone.

</Prose>

## How I approached it

<Prose>

I started with interviews on both sides of the counter — clients, RMs, operations, and digital support — to map where the friction actually lived rather than where the business assumed it did. Two pictures lined up: clients felt powerless over trivial tasks, and the internal teams knew these requests were routine and repetitive but still had to touch every one.

Then, before drawing anything, I ran a **workshop with stakeholders** to pressure-test the problem space — mapping the card tasks clients actually did against the problems they actually hit, so we designed for real behaviour, not an assumed feature list.

</Prose>

<ImageFigure
  src={`${IMAGES}/04-workshop.png`}
  alt="A whiteboard from the discovery workshop: clustered sticky notes under Usage and Problem columns, with ‘How might we…’ questions written alongside for lost cards, managing multiple cards, changing limits, forgotten PINs and payment errors."
  caption="Mapping usage against real problems — the input that decided what to build first, and what to leave for later."
/>

<Prose>

That mapping is where the **scope decision** got made. Clients hit a long tail of card problems, but four rose to the top on frequency and on how cleanly they could be solved self-service:

- **Limit change** — the single most common request
- **Block / unblock** — urgent and time-sensitive by nature
- **Card removal** — rare, but entirely manual today
- **Payments abroad** — recurring friction for travelling clients

Everything else stayed out of v1 on purpose. Choosing four and defending the boundary mattered as much as the design itself.

**One boundary worth naming:** I owned the client-facing design across those flows and the card landing page. The routing and limit *policy*, the card-management back office, and the identity/authentication service were the bank's systems — engineering owned whether the app could even reach them, and the entire self-service premise rode on that access being there. Knowing exactly where my slice started and ended shaped every trade-off below.

</Prose>

## The hard part was translation, not requirements

<Prose>

I'll be honest about where the difficulty was on this one. The *requirement* was never ambiguous: make card management self-service, keep it bank-grade secure. The challenge was entirely in the **design** — turning a task that had always been mediated by a person, with all the verification and reassurance that person quietly provided, into a screen a client trusts enough to act on alone.

Two surfaces carried most of that weight, and I explored both widely before committing:

**The card landing page** had to answer a client's first two questions at a glance — *what can I do here, and is my card okay?* — without burying the state under a menu.

**The limit-change interaction** had to hold a surprising amount: how much, for how long, whether it's permanent or temporary, and proof that it's really you — all without feeling like the phone call it replaced.

</Prose>

## The design decisions

<DecisionList
  heading="The design decisions"
  eyebrow="And what each one traded off"
  icon="sliders"
  intro={<>Every decision below is the same principle applied at a different level of risk: keep the low-risk actions instant, and spend the client's attention only where a mistake would actually cost them.</>}
  coda={{

    body: <>Underneath all of it, the interface had to hold states the phone call used to absorb: a blocked card, a limit that's only temporary, missing contact details, an identity that still needs proving. One decision per screen, and the card's current state visible at every step.</>,
  }}
>

<DecisionBlock
  index="01"
  tag="Interaction"
  title="The landing page shows state first, actions second"
  media={{
    src: `${IMAGES}/08-manage-card-final.png`,
    alt: 'The final Manage card landing: the card itself, an active/blocked status line, Card details and Block, then live limits with progress bars, then payment toggles, then Delete card.',
  }}
  tradeoff={{
    considered: 'A flat menu of card actions — the conventional pattern.',
    why: (
      <>
        A menu hides the one thing clients open the screen to see: <strong>is my card okay, and what's left on it?</strong> It
        also flattens risk — listing “view details” and “raise my limit by €30k” as equal rows. Leading with live
        state, then ordering actions by how often they're used, answered the real first question.
      </>
    ),
  }}
>

The landing page opens with the card and a plain status line — *this card is active* — then live payment and cash-withdrawal limits as progress bars, then management actions, and only then the destructive ones. It reads top to bottom as *reassurance → routine → rare*.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock
  index="02"
  tag="Interaction"
  title="Show current usage and the ceiling before any input"
  media={{
    src: `${IMAGES}/12-limit-context.png`,
    alt: 'The limit context card shown before input: “Remaining balance €5.750,00” with a progress bar reading “You spent €29.430 out of €30.000 within your current payment limit.”',
    caption: 'Context before the field — what you’ve used and how high you’re allowed to go — so the number a client enters is an informed one.',
  }}
  tradeoff={{
    considered: 'Dropping the client straight onto an empty amount field, the fastest path to “done”.',
    why: (
      <>
        The most common source of errors — and follow-up support calls — was clients setting a limit whose
        <strong> bounds they didn't understand</strong>. Showing current spend against the permitted ceiling{' '}
        <em>before</em> the field turns a guess into an informed number, and pre-empts the call that used to follow.
      </>
    ),
  }}
>

Before the client types anything, the limit screen shows what they've already used and the ceiling they're allowed to reach. It's the same "state first" instinct as the landing page, applied to the riskiest input in the feature — the moment a wrong number actually has consequences.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock
  index="03"
  tag="Security"
  title="Authenticate at the point of commitment — not on the way in"
  media={{
    src: `${IMAGES}/09-manage-payments.png`,
    alt: 'The Manage payments section: simple on/off toggles for Remote payment, Cash withdrawal and Payment abroad, with no authentication step.',
  }}
  tradeoff={{
    considered: 'Gating the whole feature behind a verification wall — the compliance-safe instinct to lock every action.',
    why: (
      <>
        Uniform friction would have rebuilt the exact wait we were removing. Most card actions are low-risk and
        reversible; a few aren't. So I put the identity step where the <strong>commitment</strong> is — raising a
        limit, deleting a card — and left browsing, viewing and toggling free.
      </>
    ),
  }}
>

Viewing card state and flipping low-risk controls — remote payment, cash withdrawal, payments abroad — happen instantly, because they're reversible and carry little exposure. The identity check appears only when a client is about to do something that would actually cost them if it were the wrong person. Same feature, friction calibrated to the stakes.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock
  index="04"
  tag="Compliance"
  title="Default the limit change to temporary, not permanent"
  media={{
    src: `${IMAGES}/10-limit-change-final.png`,
    alt: 'The limit-change flow across screens: enter a new amount within a shown ceiling, a summary of current versus new limit with an end date, a 5-digit PIN identity step, and a success confirmation.',
    caption: 'Enter an amount within the ceiling → confirm the summary → prove identity → done. The end date is part of the request, not an afterthought.',
  }}
  tradeoff={{
    considered: 'A single permanent “new limit” field, the simplest possible control.',
    why: (
      <>
        Watching the real requests, most limit bumps are for one reason — a large purchase, a trip — not a permanent
        change. A permanent-only field either over-exposes the client indefinitely or forces a <em>second</em> call
        to put it back. Foregrounding a temporary increase with an automatic end date fit what clients actually needed.
      </>
    ),
  }}
>

The flow makes duration a first-class part of the request: a client raises the limit *for seven days*, or *for thirty*, up to a clearly shown ceiling, and it reverts on its own. The card landing then carries a quiet “temporarily valid until…” marker so the temporary state is never a surprise. Identity is proven with a PIN step folded into the flow — not a separate hoop.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock
  index="05"
  tag="Interaction"
  title="Block is instant and reversible — cancelling stays with a human"
  media={{
    src: `${IMAGES}/11-block-card.png`,
    alt: 'The block flow: a confirmation sheet to block the card, and the resulting blocked state with a padlock over the card, an Unblock action, and a note to call support to fully cancel.',
  }}
  tradeoff={{
    considered: 'Treating block as a single destructive “cancel card” action.',
    why: (
      <>
        Blocking is usually a <em>panic</em> action — a card that's briefly missing. It should be instant and, just
        as importantly, <strong>reversible in seconds</strong> when the card turns up. Cancellation is destructive and
        rare, so it deliberately stays a call. Collapsing the two would make the common, urgent case as heavy as the
        rare one.
      </>
    ),
  }}
>

A client blocks a card in one confirmation, and the card visibly locks. If it resurfaces, they unblock it just as fast — no call, no wait. Permanent cancellation, which can't be undone, still routes to support on purpose.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock
  index="06"
  tag="Trust"
  title="Keep the human reachable — and hand off gracefully"
>

Self-service should never feel like abandonment — least of all in private banking, where the relationship-manager bond carries real value. So a **Call customer service shortcut sits at the bottom of every screen**: the client is always one tap from a person, even mid-flow.

The same instinct shows up in how the flows fail safely. A sensitive change needs verified contact details to confirm it's really the client — so the design checks up front. If a verified email or phone is on file, the change proceeds; if not, the flow routes cleanly to the bank to fix the details first, rather than letting someone reach the last step and hit a wall.

The vast majority of cases now resolve in the app. This decision is for the ones that don't: give them a clean exit, not a dead end.

</DecisionBlock>

</DecisionList>

## Built on Emerald

<Prose>

None of this shipped as bespoke UI. Every screen is built on **ABN AMRO's Emerald design system**, so the feature reads as part of the wider app rather than a bolt-on, and it moved into development faster because engineering was assembling known, governed components rather than interpreting one-off designs.

That's the quiet leverage of designing inside a system: my slice was the *flows and the decisions*; the system carried the consistency and cut the handoff cost. On a regulated private-banking app, that's not a constraint to work around — it's what makes the work trustworthy and shippable.

</Prose>

## What we kept with a human, on purpose

<Prose>

The honest boundary of this release: **not every card action became self-service, and that was a design choice, not a gap I missed.**

Two things still route to a person. **Permanently cancelling a card** stays with the support team — it's irreversible and identity-sensitive, and the seconds saved by automating it aren't worth the risk of getting it wrong. And **correcting stale contact details** — a wrong email or phone number on file — routes to the relationship manager rather than letting the client edit the very data used to verify them.

Both are deliberate. In a private bank, the right MVP isn't the one that automates the most actions — it's the one that automates every action where self-service is clearly *safer and faster*, and knows precisely where to stop. Naming that line is the point, not apologising for it.

</Prose>

</Prose>

## The takeaway

<Prose>

This wasn't a task made prettier. It was a **handoff chain collapsed into a flow the client runs alone** — and the discipline that made it safe to do was refusing to spread friction evenly.

The clearest lesson: on a regulated product, self-service is a question of *placement*, not addition. Put the guardrail exactly where the risk is, make everything else instant, and know — precisely — which few actions still belong with a human. That's what turns a two-hour call into a few taps without a bank giving up an ounce of control.

</Prose>
