## Project context

<Prose>

Postbox is the secure digital vault inside ABN AMRO Private Banking's apps — used here by **Bethmann Bank**, ABN AMRO's private bank in Germany — where high-net-worth clients receive, store, and share confidential documents: statements, contracts, tax papers, correspondence with their relationship manager.

For a private-banking client, these are not files to skim and forget. They are the documents an accountant asks for, a notary needs, a tax deadline depends on. Retrieval isn't a nice-to-have — it's the whole job of the vault.

</Prose>

## The business goal

<Prose>

Postbox was already one of the most-used features in the private-banking apps — but it was underperforming the trust clients place in it. Documents were filed the bank's way, by regulatory category, so clients couldn't find what they came for and fell back on their relationship manager for basic retrieval.

The goal: **raise the digital experience of the whole private-banking proposition**, with Postbox as a lever. Make the vault self-service — a client can find and export any document without a phone call — and lift the NPS that a banker-dependent archive was quietly holding down.

</Prose>

## The problem

<Prose>

High-net-worth clients **struggled to find and export the documents that mattered** — and when they couldn't, they contacted their relationship manager to do it for them. That drove up support tickets and RM complaints, and turned a self-service feature into a manual one.

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="user">The page looks nice, but it's difficult to navigate — and the technology behind it is not OK.</PullQuote>

<Prose>

</Prose>

## Discover

<Prose>

One real constraint shaped the whole approach: in this private-banking context, **direct interviews with clients weren't possible** — the product managers couldn't open that channel. So instead of guessing, I triangulated three evidence sources that *were* available — behavioural analytics, an in-product feedback stream, and secondary research — and built the personas from those. No direct access doesn't have to mean no understanding.

</Prose>

## Define

<Prose>

I distilled the evidence into **two personas** and a **customer journey map** — the artifacts that made the drop-off points concrete and kept the team anchored to real behaviour rather than opinion.

</Prose>

<Prose>

Mapping his path exposed three drop-off points, each of which became a fix that carried the most weight:

- **Search that actually works** — real-time results across every folder, not a lookup confined to one branch of the tree
- **Documents you can recognise** — clearer naming, and download limits communicated up front instead of discovered at failure
- **Actions ranked by importance** — the things clients do most (find, export) prioritised over the things the taxonomy happened to surface

</Prose>

<Prose>

This phase bridged business requirements and user needs — moving from constraints to layouts without losing either.

I ran **stakeholder workshops** to brainstorm structures that simplified navigation while holding the bank's security and compliance standards intact. Low-fidelity sketches turned the ideas into something we could react to, refined against business feedback before a single pixel was polished.

</Prose>

## Usability testing

<Prose>

I validated the design across **two web versions and one mobile-app version** — gathering qualitative feedback and ratings to decide direction with evidence, not preference.

</Prose>

<ImageFigure
  wide
  src={`${IMAGES}/06-usability-test.png`}
  alt="The tested Postbox versions — two desktop layouts and two mobile layouts — annotated with tester feedback: what held up (clear search/filter, easy Inbox/Archive navigation, clear action buttons, an undo for a mis-moved file) and what testers asked for (search bar placed up front, clearer archive icon, less filtering when names are clear)."
  caption="Both versions annotated with real tester feedback — what held up, and what the testers told me to change."
/>

<Prose>

**Version 1 won decisively** — 60% of testers preferred it at an average **8.5/10**, against 40% and **7.5** for Version 2. But the most useful finding was one the prototype hadn't anticipated: clients wanted **control over how the vault reached them** — to choose the *channel* (email, SMS, push) and the *frequency*, like one weekly summary of everything unread instead of a mail per document. That became **customisable notification preferences**, added on the strength of testing rather than assumption.

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="principle">
  A search box no one trusts is worse than no search box — it fails at the exact moment the client needs it most. Earn the search, and the folders stop mattering.
</PullQuote>

Buttons and functions are ordered by **client importance, not taxonomy convenience** — retrieval and export first, with **bulk download and archive** promoted to primary actions. Select several documents and export them as one ZIP straight through the native share sheet — the exact "share with my family office" job clients described, done in one pass rather than one file at a time.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock
  index="04"

>

Surfaced entirely by testing: the old daily email "feels like spam — you cannot deactivate these mails." So the redesign lets clients choose **how** the vault reaches them — email, SMS or push — and **how often**, down to a single weekly summary of everything unread. Control the old vault never offered, added because clients asked for it under test.

</DecisionBlock>

## The takeaway

<Prose>

In a domain as complex as private banking, the most valuable thing you can give a client is their **time back**. Postbox wasn't short of features — it was short of a path to the one document a client actually needed. Reducing clutter and committing to a search-first layout made the whole vault more effective than any new capability would have.

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="principle">
  Simplicity is strength: don't add a better feature — clear the path to the one that already matters.
</PullQuote>

<Prose>

The other lesson was procedural: **seek feedback early and continually.** Engaging stakeholders in workshops and testing multiple versions of the prototype up front prevented costly late-stage changes and surfaced needs — like client-controlled notifications — that no amount of desk analysis would have. Constant feedback is what let the final product clear both the security bar and the client's expectations at once.

</Prose>
