## The problem

<Prose>

On paper this was a migration: lift a legacy desktop terminal into the cloud. But the terminal wasn't just old — it was *physically trapping* the people who depended on it.

**The counter-lock bottleneck.** Managers could only do admin work at the physical front counter — the one machine, in the one spot, that ran the terminal. Every staffing task competed directly with serving customers standing at that same counter.

</Prose>

<ImageFigure
  src={`${IMAGES}/02-counter-lock.png`}
  alt="A post-office manager at the front counter, split between a customer waiting to be served and the legacy staff-management terminal — the single point where admin work could happen."
  caption="The counter-lock: admin work and customer service fought over the same desk, the same minute."
/>

<Prose>

That single constraint produced everything else:

- **A shadow system.** With no remote access, managers copied private staff details into personal paper notebooks so they could do the work at home — sensitive data walking out the door, off any secure system
- **Interrupted service.** Any staffing change meant stepping away from the customer in front of them
- **Rehiring friction.** Old database rules blocked managers from easily re-onboarding seasonal staff — a recurring, predictable need the system treated as an exception

The instinct with a mandatory migration is to port the thing faithfully and move on. But the paper notebooks were the tell: the real failure wasn't the technology, it was *where* the work was allowed to happen.

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="principle">
  This was never just a migration. The terminal's real defect was that it bound critical work to one physical location — so the design goal wasn't "recreate the terminal in a browser," it was "unbind the work from the counter."
</PullQuote>

## How I approached it

<Prose>

One month, a hard deadline, and a system with real legacy constraints underneath it. I ran this as a lead UX process built for rapid, grounded execution — not a long discovery, but never a blind one either.

The move that mattered most: I went to the stores. During physical site visits and face-to-face interviews, one thing became unambiguous — managers weren't using paper because they *preferred* it. They used it because the system was inaccessible anywhere but the counter.

</Prose>

<ImageFigure
  wide
  src={`${IMAGES}/03-site-visit.png`}

<Prose>

That visit reset the platform priority. **Mobile access was the #1 request** — not a nicer desktop screen. So the portal became mobile-first, not desktop-first-then-shrunk. The whole layout strategy pivoted on a finding I could only have gotten by standing where the work happens.

</Prose>

## The insight that changed the scope

<Prose>

The most important decision wasn't a screen — it was a scope call about the shadow system.

The easy read was: "managers keep paper notebooks, so digitize the list and the notebooks disappear." But the field interviews said something subtler. Managers didn't want paper for its own sake — they wanted a **physical copy they could rely on** when they were away from the counter, offline, or handling an emergency. Take that away with nothing in its place and the notebooks come right back.

So I didn't just digitize the list. I designed a secure **Download / Export** feature into the portal.

</Prose>

<ImageFigure
  src={`${IMAGES}/04-export-feature.png`}
  alt="The portal's staff list with a secure Download / Export action — producing a physical-copy export while the live record stays in the cloud as the source of truth."
  caption="Export gives managers the physical copy they actually needed — while the source of truth stays digital and secure."
/>

<Prose>

That one addition is what genuinely *eliminated* the shadow system instead of just competing with it. It met the real need — a backup they could hold — while keeping the **source of truth** digital, current, and secure. The paper habit had somewhere legitimate to go.

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="principle">
  You don't kill a workaround by banning it. You kill it by understanding the need underneath it and serving that need better — here, a secure export beat an insecure notebook on its own terms.
</PullQuote>

## The design decisions

<Prose>

Post offices are high-stress, high-distraction retail floors — often being used one-handed, on a small screen, between customers. Every interaction choice below came from designing for *that* environment, not a calm desktop.

</Prose>

<DecisionBlock
  index="01"
  title="Role inheritance — one choice, not two"
  media={{
    src: `${IMAGES}/05-role-inheritance.png`,
    alt: 'The add-user form where selecting the "Admin" role automatically toggles on "Standard User" permissions underneath it.',
  }}
>

Selecting **"Admin"** automatically toggles on **"Standard User"** permissions beneath it — because an admin is always also a standard user. A small logic tweak, but during hiring peaks it removed a whole class of clicks and configuration errors: managers couldn't accidentally create an admin who lacked the base permissions to actually work.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock
  index="02"
  title="Full-row click targets"
  media={{
    src: `${IMAGES}/06-full-row-click.png`,
    alt: 'A staff table on a small mobile screen where the entire row is a tap target, not just the name link.',
  }}
  tradeoff={{
    considered: 'A conventional table with a small "edit" link or icon per row.',
    why: (
      <>
        On a phone, between customers, a tiny target is a missed tap and a lost second. Making the whole
        row tappable was the higher-reliability choice for the environment this actually runs in.
      </>
    ),
  }}
>

I made the **entire row** a tap target across every staff table (Fitts's Law in practice — the bigger and closer the target, the faster and safer the hit). For a busy manager on a small mobile screen in a high-distraction space, it means fast, error-free navigation instead of hunting for a link.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock
  index="03"
  title="The 7-day safety net for deletes"
>

The legacy behavior was a **hard delete** — remove a staff member and the record was gone, no recovery. In a system managers now run one-handed between customers, that's a data-loss incident waiting to happen.

So I replaced it with a **soft delete on a 7-day recovery window**, and designed the confirmation to make the stakes unmistakable: *"The account will be permanently removed in 7 days. You can undo the deletion anytime."*

The modal itself does deliberate work: the **Remove** button is high-contrast **red** to signal the seriousness of the action, while **Cancel** stays visually neutral. That asymmetry forces a conscious *stop-and-think* moment — you can't fat-finger your way through a destructive action.

</DecisionBlock>

</DecisionList>

## The takeaway

<Prose>

This project looked like a technical chore — port a legacy terminal to the cloud. The design work was refusing to treat it that way.

The terminal's real defect wasn't that it was old; it was that it bound critical work to one physical spot, which is why the paper notebooks existed at all. Once the goal became *operational freedom* rather than *faithful migration*, everything followed — mobile-first because that's what managers asked for, a secure export because that's what the notebooks were really for, and a recoverable delete because the system now runs in someone's hand between customers.

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="principle">
  The best migration doesn't reproduce the old system faithfully — it fixes the constraint the old system forced people to live with. Unbind the work, and the workarounds disappear on their own.
</PullQuote>
