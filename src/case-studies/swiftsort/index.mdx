## Project landscape

<Prose>

SwiftSort sits at a specific inflection point in the parcel logistics value chain: the **terminal sort** — the moment upstream routing data must become a physical action on a warehouse floor. Understanding where this slice began and ended shaped every design trade-off.

</Prose>

<Prose>

**Operating environment:** Nordic parcel terminals — cold, noisy, glove-required. Workers operate forklifts with mounted tablets (sometimes out of arm's reach) or carry handheld TC57 scanners. The workforce spans experienced operators with years of internalized route knowledge to new hires with no mental model at all.

**Key constraint:** Any intervention had to run on hardware already deployed — no new devices, no floor layout changes, no additional training cycles. The design had to reduce interaction cost to near-zero while respecting existing motor patterns.

**Users:** Forklift operators and terminal sorters, working under time pressure with gloves on. Their expertise lives in muscle memory and route recall — not in screens.

</Prose>

## The problem

<Prose>

Imagine standing on a warehouse floor as parcels pour in. Every box looks the same — nothing tells you which has to leave *today* and which can wait. You just have to *know*, from experience, which gate each postal code maps to and in what order to stack.

That was the operating reality. Sorting relied entirely on tacit domain knowledge — undocumented heuristics that existed only in experienced workers' heads. During a contextual inquiry, one operator summarized the entire system gap:

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="user">I just memorize it. When I forget, I ask someone.</PullQuote>

<Prose>

That single statement encapsulated the core design problem. Critical operational knowledge existed as internalized mental models — never externalized, never systematized — and it degraded with every shift change and was lost entirely with turnover.

**What that cost the business:**

- **Degraded throughput under load** — each parcel incurred cognitive overhead for heuristic-based route recall, compounding across hundreds of parcels per shift
- **Priority misrouting** — no visual differentiation at the sorting decision point; time-sensitive and standard goods converged at the same gate
- **Steep learnability curve** — new operators lacked the internalized mental model, creating a multi-week ramp before they could sort independently
- **Cross-site variance** — throughput tracked tenure and individual expertise, not system capability; no two terminals performed alike

The obvious interventions — new equipment, more training, a redesigned floor — would have been slow, costly, and hard to scale across sites. The highest-leverage move was to externalize the decision, not optimize around it.

**The reframe that shaped everything:**

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="principle">
  Sorting was never a screen problem. It was a *decision-architecture* problem. Workers were performing system-level cognition — mapping, remembering, sequencing — manually, under time pressure. The design opportunity was not faster information display. It was **externalizing the decision entirely** — moving the cognitive work from the operator to the system.
</PullQuote>

## How I approached it

<Prose>

I started on the terminal floor, not in Figma — running contextual inquiries across shifts: shadowing forklift operators through full sorting cycles, observing how goods and pallets actually got stacked, and conducting semi-structured interviews with terminal managers and ops leads to surface the informal workarounds that no documentation captured.

The research goal was not to invent a new workflow, but to map the existing one with enough fidelity to find the behavior that *already worked* — and design the system around it.

**Scope boundary:** SwiftSort does not decide *where* a parcel goes — planners set routing upstream. My design slice was the worker-facing decision layer: the moment upstream data must become a physical action on a noisy floor. Defining that boundary early prevented scope drift and kept every trade-off anchored.

</Prose>

## The moment that changed the design

<Prose>

The most important design decision in this project did not come from the brief. It came from re-examining a usability failure in recorded session data.

</Prose>

### The wrong direction

<Prose>

During proof-of-concept testing, forklift operators kept struggling to reach a confirmation button on the mounted tablet — bolted in the only spot it physically fit, which was outside comfortable reach during operation.

The team converged on the obvious: **make the button bigger, move the tablet, add a second one.** We spent days iterating on placement — and no one questioned whether the affordance should require reach at all.

</Prose>

### The reframe

<Prose>

I opened the session recording from that day and reviewed it the next morning — and identified what I had missed during live observation: **every worker was already wearing a ProGlove scanner.** Activated, embedded in their motor pattern. They were not reaching for the tablet because they wanted to touch it — the system was forcing a tap confirmation on a device outside their natural interaction zone.

So I reframed the constraint: *what if the confirmation affordance did not require reach at all?*

I proposed a **static QR code** as the last card in the drop list — scan it with the ProGlove already on your hand, *or* tap it. On a tablet that is sometimes out of arm's reach, the scan path makes physical proximity irrelevant: an action you can trigger from wherever you are standing.

**Research finding that drove this decision:** Contextual inquiry revealed that barcode scanning was already an automatic, glove-compatible motor pattern. Building on that existing behavior — rather than introducing a new input modality — eliminated the learnability cost entirely.

Getting the team there took more than the idea. The button-placement debate had days of momentum — so rather than argue, I brought everyone back to the recording and let them watch the glove do the work. The question shifted from "where does the button go" to "why is reach the constraint," and alignment followed.

I will be honest: it was a hypothesis, not a proven answer — the kind of idea that is either elegant or redundant, and I could not tell which from a meeting room. It hinged on one thing I could not settle at a desk: would a worker mid-shift, gloves on, actually reach for the scan? That question sent it to the floor instead of a polished mockup.

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="principle">
  <strong>The insight came from watching a recording a second time — not from the brief.</strong> The fix wasn't a bigger button or a better mount. It was giving the worker a second way in, so the mounting distance stopped being a problem to solve.
</PullQuote>

## Working with engineering

<Prose>

The static-QR idea only worked because engineering pressure-tested it early.

Engineering's first instinct was a **dynamic QR** — a unique code generated for every scanned parcel. Within a day, they flagged the problems: significant backend complexity, scalability concerns, and a timeline we couldn't hit.

We landed on **static QR** together — and it turned out to be not just simpler but genuinely *better*:

- **Reliable** — no backend to fail, works offline
- **Scalable** — print, attach, deploy; the same code works at every terminal
- **Zero-config** — operations can place codes anywhere without involving IT

The constraint didn't compromise the design — it *improved* it: the thing easiest to build was also the most robust on the floor.

</Prose>

<DecisionBlock
  index="03"
  title="The screen mirrors the physical stack (LIFO)"
  media={{
    src: `${IMAGES}/10-lifo-layout.png`,

**Research link:** Behavioral observation across shifts revealed that forklift drivers drop off the **most recently scanned** item first roughly 95-99% of the time — a consistent LIFO (last-in, first-out) pattern.

So the information architecture mirrors the physical stack: the **latest scanned pallet renders as the largest card at the top**, with smaller cards beneath. The screen order matches the forklift order — zero cognitive translation required.

*The honest edge case:* that leaves 1-5% of cases where last-scanned is not next-dropped. Workers can scan any card directly to override — but the override rate is the one assumption I would want to instrument over a full peak season before calling it settled.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock index="04" title="Priority surfaces at the moment of sorting">

**Research link:** Field observation and dispatch data review revealed that priority information existed upstream but never reached the sorting decision point — the worker had no visual signal that a parcel was time-sensitive.

Time-sensitive shipments now appear with a distinct accent treatment, floating to the top of the stack — **before** the worker decides where to place the parcel. Moving the priority signal from dispatch to the sorting moment is why urgent and low-priority goods stopped mixing at the gate.

*Not in the brief. Surfaced through research synthesis.*

</DecisionBlock>

## One solution, two form factors

<Prose>

The QR-as-button was never meant to be a universal pattern — it solved a *specific* ergonomic constraint: a tablet out of reach on a forklift. Some workers do not use a mounted tablet at all; they carry a handheld **TC57** with a built-in scanner, already in hand. For that form factor, the QR is unnecessary — confirmation is a direct tap on the green **Drop** button.

This is context-sensitive interaction design — the same task, two input strategies matched to two physical realities:

</Prose>

<Prose>

This is the deliberate design choice I would defend most: the elegant solution was matched to the context that *needed* it, and withheld from the one that did not. A QR on the TC57 would have been a solution in search of a problem — over-designing for a constraint that does not exist in that form factor. Match the interaction model to the device reality; do not standardize one pattern across divergent contexts.

</Prose>

## A second win, surfaced by the business

<Prose>

The QR insight came from watching the floor. This one came from the other direction — a discovery session with the business.

Going through how the terminals actually ran with managers and ops leads, a pattern surfaced that observation alone had missed: when a driver had **several loose items headed to the same destination**, the app made them handle each one separately — a card per item, scanned and dropped one at a time. Quietly repetitive, and slower than it needed to be at volume.

The idea didn't come from me alone. In a brainstorm with the **tech lead and product owner**, we shaped the high-level solution together — let a driver **group everything bound for one destination and drop it in a single scan**, triggered by a barcode embedded in the primary card. Engineering pressure-tested feasibility in the room; the PO kept us anchored on what mattered most; I designed the flow around both.

</Prose>

## How the work got made

<Prose>

AI compressed the divergent phase of exploration; the field decided what converged. **Figma Make** generated six LIFO card layout variants rapidly — and I evaluated each against one usability heuristic: could a worker parse it at arm's length, in motion, wearing gloves? The variants that performed well on a desktop screen were the first to fail this test. **MS Copilot** clustered raw field notes into thematic affinity groups (that is how the "priority at the sorting moment" insight surfaced); **Banani** structured the stacking interaction flow before I committed it to high-fidelity in Figma.

Net effect: roughly **30% faster early exploration** — but every decision that shipped was validated through on-site testing, not generated output.

</Prose>

## Outcomes

<Prose>

These outcomes are not self-assessed. The faster onboarding and reduced gate errors were surfaced by **terminal managers** in stakeholder reviews — operational leaders citing the system as evidence of improvement. That is the strongest form of validation: the people running the operation found it significant enough to report upward unprompted.

Beyond throughput, the *operational model* shifted. Delivery priority became visible at the sorting decision point, so time-sensitive shipments met their departure windows; low-volume routes could be consolidated instead of dispatched half-empty — making fast-delivery commitments more scalable without additional headcount.

</Prose>

## What we shipped knowingly incomplete

<Prose>

We went live as an MVP with one known, unresolved gap: **the drop-off location editing flow is not yet intuitive, and parcel grouping does not behave as cleanly as the design intends.** We identified this before launch — and shipped deliberately, treating it as a learning gap rather than a blocker.

Drop-off locations come from planners upstream, and the data behavior at production volume was unpredictable from a design tool. Rather than model assumptions into the grouping logic, we shipped the core workflow and instrumented the first release to *learn how the data behaves in production* — then iterate grouping and location-editing against observed patterns, not hypotheses.

This is the trade-off I would defend most: a scoped MVP with a named, understood gap that generates real behavioral data outperforms a "complete" design built on assumptions about data you do not control.

</Prose>

## The takeaway

<Prose>

SwiftSort was not a UI redesign. It was a redesign of **decision architecture** on a warehouse floor.

By externalizing cognitive work from operators to the system — triggered by motor patterns they already possessed — sorting shifted from *tacit-knowledge-dependent* to *system-guided*, from *tenure-correlated* to *scalable from day one*.

The clearest lesson: in operational systems, high-impact design is rarely about adding features or surfaces. It is about **removing cognitive constraints** — and the highest-value removal in this project came from reviewing session data a second time and questioning why *physical reach* had to be the constraint we designed around at all.

</Prose>
