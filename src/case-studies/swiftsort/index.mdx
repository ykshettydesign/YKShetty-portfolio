*The whole project ran on one principle:*

  <strong>Find the behavior that already exists, and build the system around it</strong> — so workers have less to learn, not more.

## The problem

<Prose>

Imagine standing on a warehouse floor as parcels pour in. Every box looks the same — nothing tells you which has to leave *today* and which can wait. You just have to *know*, from experience, which gate each postal code maps to and in what order to stack.

That was the daily reality. Sorting depended entirely on what workers had memorized. When I asked one operator how he kept it all straight, he shrugged:

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="user">I just memorize it. When I forget, I ask someone.</PullQuote>

<Prose>

That one sentence is the whole problem. Critical operational knowledge wasn't in the system — it was in people's heads, and it walked out the door every time someone changed shifts or left.

**What that cost the business:**

- **Slow throughput** during peak hours, because every parcel required a mental lookup
- **Frequent errors** — urgent and low-priority goods mixed at the gate, so time-sensitive deliveries missed their trucks
- **Painful onboarding** — a new hire couldn't be useful until they'd memorized the routes, which took weeks
- **Inconsistent terminals** — experienced operators were fast; everyone else was slow, and no two sites ran the same way

Solving this the obvious way — new equipment, more training, a redesigned floor — would have been slow, costly, and hard to roll out across sites. The stronger play was to handle more volume with what the terminals already had.

**The reframe that shaped everything:**

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="principle">
  Sorting was never a screen problem. It was a *decision* problem. Workers were doing system-level thinking — mapping, remembering, sequencing — manually, under pressure. The real opportunity wasn't to show them information faster. It was to move the thinking out of their heads and into the system.
</PullQuote>

## How I approached it

<Prose>

I started on the terminal floor, not in Figma — shadowing forklift operators through full shifts, watching how goods and pallets actually got stacked, and sitting with terminal managers and ops leads to find where the informal workarounds lived.

The job wasn't to invent a new way to sort, but to find the behavior that *already worked* — the principle that drove every decision below.

**One boundary worth naming:** SwiftSort doesn't decide *where* a parcel goes — planners set routing upstream. My slice was the worker-facing decision layer: the moment upstream data becomes a physical action on a noisy floor. Knowing exactly where that slice started and ended shaped every trade-off below.

</Prose>

## The moment that changed the design

<Prose>

The most important decision in this project didn't come from the brief. It came from watching a mistake.

</Prose>

### The wrong direction

<Prose>

During proof-of-concept testing, forklift drivers kept struggling to reach a button on the mounted tablet — bolted in the only spot it physically fit, which wasn't a spot a human arm could reach mid-operation.

The team's instinct was everyone's: **make the button bigger, move the tablet, add a second one.** We spent days on placement — and no one asked whether the button should exist at all.

</Prose>

### The reframe

<Prose>

I opened the session recording from that day and watched it again the next morning — and saw what I'd missed live: **every worker was already wearing a ProGlove scanner.** Activated, in the workflow. They weren't reaching for the tablet because they wanted to touch it — the system was asking them to confirm with a tap.

So I asked a different question: *what if the button didn't have to be reachable?*

I proposed a **static QR code** as the last card in the list — scan it with the ProGlove already on your hand, *or* tap it. On a tablet that's sometimes out of arm's reach, the scan path makes reach irrelevant: a button you can trigger from wherever you're standing.

Getting the team there took more than the idea. The button debate had days of momentum — so rather than argue placement, I brought everyone back to the recording and let them watch the glove do the work. The question shifted from "where does the button go" to "why is reach the constraint," and the room moved on its own.

I'll be honest: it was a hunch, not a proven answer — the kind of idea that's either elegant or redundant, and I couldn't tell which from a meeting room. It hinged on one thing I couldn't settle at a desk: would a worker mid-shift, gloves on, actually reach for the scan? That question sent it to the floor instead of a polished mockup.

</Prose>

<PullQuote variant="principle">
  <strong>The insight came from watching a recording a second time — not from the brief.</strong> The fix wasn't a bigger button or a better mount. It was giving the worker a second way in, so the mounting distance stopped being a problem to solve.
</PullQuote>

## Working with engineering

<Prose>

The static-QR idea only worked because engineering pressure-tested it early.

Engineering's first instinct was a **dynamic QR** — a unique code generated for every scanned parcel. Within a day, they flagged the problems: significant backend complexity, scalability concerns, and a timeline we couldn't hit.

We landed on **static QR** together — and it turned out to be not just simpler but genuinely *better*:

- **Reliable** — no backend to fail, works offline
- **Scalable** — print, attach, deploy; the same code works at every terminal
- **Zero-config** — operations can place codes anywhere without involving IT

The constraint didn't compromise the design — it *improved* it: the thing easiest to build was also the most robust on the floor.

</Prose>

During research I saw that forklift drivers almost always drop off the **most recently scanned** item first — roughly 95–99% of the time, last-in is first-out.

So the design puts the **latest scanned pallet as the largest card at the top**, with smaller cards beneath. The order on screen matches the order on the forklift — no mental translation.

*The honest edge case:* that leaves 1–5% of cases where last-scanned *isn't* next-dropped. Workers can scan any card directly to override — but the override rate is the one assumption I'd want to instrument over a full peak season before calling it settled.

</DecisionBlock>

<DecisionBlock index="04" title="Priority surfaces at the moment of sorting">

Time-sensitive shipments now appear with a distinct accent treatment, floating to the top of the stack — **before** the worker decides where to place the parcel.

The priority signal used to live in dispatch, far upstream. Moving it to the sorting moment is why urgent and low-priority goods stopped mixing at the gate.

*Not in the brief. Found in the field.*

</DecisionBlock>

</DecisionList>

## One solution, two form factors

<Prose>

The QR-as-button was never meant to be everywhere — it solved a *specific* physical problem: a tablet out of reach on a forklift. Some workers don't use a mounted tablet at all; they carry a handheld **TC57** with a built-in scanner, already in hand. So that version **drops the QR entirely** and confirms with a tap on the green **Drop** button.

The same task, designed twice:

</Prose>

This is the part I'm most deliberate about: the clever solution was matched to the context that *needed* it, and withheld from the one that didn't. A QR on the TC57 would have been a solution in search of a problem. Match the input to the device's reality; don't standardize one pattern everywhere.

</Prose>

## A second win, surfaced by the business

<Prose>

The QR insight came from watching the floor. This one came from the other direction — a discovery session with the business.

Going through how the terminals actually ran with managers and ops leads, a pattern surfaced that observation alone had missed: when a driver had **several loose items headed to the same destination**, the app made them handle each one separately — a card per item, scanned and dropped one at a time. Quietly repetitive, and slower than it needed to be at volume.

The idea didn't come from me alone. In a brainstorm with the **tech lead and product owner**, we shaped the high-level solution together — let a driver **group everything bound for one destination and drop it in a single scan**, triggered by a barcode embedded in the primary card. Engineering pressure-tested feasibility in the room; the PO kept us anchored on what mattered most; I designed the flow around both.

</Prose>

It's the clearest example of a decision that came from the room, not the brief — and the reason I treat discovery meetings as design research, not status updates.

</Prose>

## How the work got made

<Prose>

AI compressed the slow early phase; the field decided what shipped. **Figma Make** generated six LIFO card layouts fast — and I killed most against one test: could a worker read it at arm's length, in motion, wearing gloves? The variants that looked richer on a desktop screen were the first to go. **MS Copilot** clustered raw field notes into themes (that's how the "priority at the sorting moment" finding surfaced); **Banani** structured the stacking flow before I committed it to Figma.

Net effect: roughly **30% faster early exploration** — but every decision that shipped came from on-site testing, not a generated mockup.

</Prose>

## Testing on the floor

<Prose>

Not validated in a design tool — validated on Zebra devices, on the terminal floor, twice.

**Round 1 failed in useful ways.** Workers struggled with controls *while moving*, stacking interactions were too fiddly, and scan feedback wasn't clear enough. Nothing about the concept was wrong — the execution was too fine-grained for a glove and a moving forklift.

**I iterated:** stronger visual hierarchy, clearer color coding, higher visibility for the latest scan, bigger targets, simpler interactions.

**Round 2 confirmed the fix.** Workers moved through sorting noticeably faster, preferred the guided flow over memorization, and stacking got measurably more consistent — holding up under real movement, real gloves, real noise. That's what let us ship.

</Prose>

## Outcomes

<Prose>

The wins up top aren't self-assessment — the faster onboarding and fewer gate errors came from **terminal managers**, who raised them with business stakeholders as proof the system was working. That's the validation I trust most: the people running the operation found it real enough to report upward.

Beyond that, the *shape* of the operation changed. Delivery priority became visible at the moment it mattered, so time-sensitive shipments left on time; and low-volume routes could be consolidated instead of dispatched half-empty — which made fast-delivery commitments more scalable.

</Prose>

## What we shipped knowingly incomplete

<Prose>

We went live as an MVP with one known, unresolved issue: **changing a drop-off location isn't intuitive yet, and parcels don't group as cleanly as they should.** We knew this before launch — and shipped anyway, on purpose.

Drop-off locations come from planners upstream, and we couldn't predict how that data would behave at real volume. So rather than guess at the grouping model in a design tool, we shipped the core workflow and used the first release to *learn how the data behaves in the wild* — then optimize grouping and location-editing against real patterns, not assumptions.

It's the trade-off I'd defend hardest: an MVP with a named, understood gap that generates real learning beats a "complete" design built on guesses about data we didn't control.

</Prose>

## The takeaway

<Prose>

SwiftSort wasn't a UI redesign. It was a redesign of **how a decision gets made** on a warehouse floor.

By moving the thinking out of workers' heads and into the system — triggered by behavior they already had — sorting shifted from *memory-based* to *system-guided*, from *experience-dependent* to *scalable*.

The clearest lesson: in operational systems, great design usually isn't about adding features. It's about **removing constraints** — and the best removal in this project came from watching a recording twice and asking why *reach* had to be the thing we designed around at all.

</Prose>
